Subtractors

A single control line and one XOR gate per bit are sufficient to make an adder perform subtraction as well.

Part 1

Subtraction without a subtractor

A dedicated subtraction circuit would approximately double the arithmetic hardware.

A subtractor may be constructed directly, propagating borrows in place of carries. Such circuits exist, but they are seldom built.

Since A − B is equivalent to A + (−B), subtraction reduces to addition with one operand negated, provided that negative values can themselves be represented as bit patterns. A single adder then performs both operations.

The representation that permits this is two’s complement, introduced in Signed Number Representations. A value is negated by inverting every bit and adding 1.

Part 2

One control line does both jobs

Both the inversion and the addition of 1 are obtained at negligible cost.

Subtraction requires two operations on the second operand: inversion of B, and the addition of 1. Both are obtained from a single control line, denoted SUB.

Inverting with XOR

Put an XOR gate on each B bit with SUB as its other input. Recall from Module 2 that XOR outputs 1 when its inputs differ:

SUBBiBi ⊕ SUB
000
011

With SUB = 0 the bit passes unchanged; with SUB = 1 it emerges inverted. Each gate therefore acts as a controlled inverter, at a cost of one gate per bit.

The +1 costs nothing

The carry-in of the least significant full adder is unused in a plain adder and is tied to 0. Connecting it to SUB instead supplies the required increment.

When SUB = 1 a 1 enters the least significant column, which is the increment that two’s complement negation requires. No additional adder or stage is needed.

result = A + (B ⊕ SUB) + SUB

With SUB = 0 this evaluates to A + B; with SUB = 1 it evaluates to A + B′ + 1, which is A − B.

Part 4

Reading the carry out as a borrow

During subtraction the carry output carries a different and useful interpretation.

When SUB = 1 the carry out is no longer an overflow signal. For unsigned operands it tells you whether the subtraction needed to borrow:

1No borrow neededA ≥ Bthe correct difference
0A borrow was neededA < Bthe difference plus 16, i.e. negative in two’s complement

A single bit produced by the subtraction therefore establishes whether one operand is at least as large as the other. The following activity develops a magnitude comparator from this result.

Some processor families invert this bit and call it a borrow flag instead. The information is identical; only the convention differs.
