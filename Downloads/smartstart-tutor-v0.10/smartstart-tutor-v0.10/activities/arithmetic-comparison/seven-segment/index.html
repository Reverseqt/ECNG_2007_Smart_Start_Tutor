Part 1

Understanding the display

A BCD-to-seven-segment decoder drives seven labelled segments, whose combinations form the decimal digits.

BCD input D C B A0101
Active segments a–g

A common-cathode, active-high display is assumed throughout: an output of 1 illuminates the corresponding segment.

Part 2

Six patterns BCD never uses

Four bits give sixteen codes. BCD only defines ten of them.

The decoder above takes four bits, so it has sixteen possible inputs — but only 0000 to 1001 mean anything in BCD. The remaining six, 1010 through 1111, are invalid BCD codes. A real BCD decoder either blanks the display for them or produces meaningless shapes.

Those same six codes are exactly the hexadecimal digits A, B, C, D, E and F. Give them sensible shapes and the same seven segments become a hexadecimal display, which is what you find on debugging hardware and old front panels.

Why two of them are lowercase

Written as capitals, B would be identical to 8, and D would be identical to 0. Neither is distinguishable on seven segments, so the convention is uppercase A C E F and lowercase b d. That is a display constraint driving a notation choice, not an arbitrary style decision.
