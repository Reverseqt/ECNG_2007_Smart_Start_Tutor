Comparators Revisited

With subtraction available, the magnitude comparator can be derived rather than assumed: to compare two numbers is to determine the sign of their difference.

Home / Arithmetic and Comparison / Comparators Revisited

Part 1

The box, reopened

The comparator introduced earlier was treated as a black box. Its interior is now examined.

In Binary Comparators the circuit was treated as a black box: two words in, three mutually exclusive outputs out. The method described there is the one used in manual comparison: compare the most significant bits, and where these are equal move right and compare the next pair.

Dedicated comparator devices operate in precisely this way. A subtractor, however, already produces almost all of the information a comparator requires.

Comparison as subtraction

To compare A and B is to enquire after the sign of A − B. If the difference is zero the operands are equal; if the subtraction required a borrow, A was the smaller. Two status flags therefore answer the question completely.

Part 2

Two flags, three answers

All three comparator outputs, derived from the status bits of the subtraction.

Run A − B through the adder–subtractor and take two signals from it:

  • Zero — every result bit is 0. One NOR gate across the result bits produces it.
  • Carry out — 1 when no borrow was needed, which for unsigned operands means A ≥ B.

The three comparator outputs follow directly:

Deriving the comparator outputs
OutputExpressionReasoning
A = BZeroThe difference is zero.
A < BCarry′A borrow was needed, so A was too small.
A > BCarry · Zero′No borrow was needed and the difference is not zero.

The complete comparator therefore consists of one inverter, one AND gate and one NOR gate, added to a subtractor provided for other purposes.

Part 4

Comparing the two designs

Both designs are correct; they differ in cost.

Dedicated comparator against comparison by subtraction
Dedicated comparatorSubtract and test flags
MethodCompare bit pairs from the most significant endCompute A − B, read the carry and zero flags
Extra hardwareA full comparator per comparisonThree gates, if an adder already exists
Best whenComparison is the only job, as in a standalone chipThe circuit already does arithmetic

Within a processor the second approach is preferred, since the arithmetic unit is present in any case and the additional cost is three gates. Instruction sets accordingly seldom provide a separate comparison unit: a compare instruction is generally a subtraction whose difference is discarded and whose status flags are retained.

Both approaches assume unsigned operands. With signed values the carry flag alone is not enough, and processors add an overflow flag so that signed comparisons can be resolved correctly.
