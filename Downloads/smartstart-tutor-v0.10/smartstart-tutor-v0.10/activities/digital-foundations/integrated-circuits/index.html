Part 1

What is an integrated circuit?

An integrated circuit is a semiconductor crystal carrying interconnected components realised as transistors.

An integrated circuit combines many components on one piece of semiconductor material. The external package protects that fragile die and makes its terminals reachable. Three things characterise any chip you might pick up: the transistor technology it is built from, the package it arrives in, and its scale of integration.

The first of these determines the electrical behaviour examined in the preceding activity, where TTL and CMOS thresholds were quoted without definition. Those families are defined below.

Part 2

What a logic family actually is

A logic family is a set of devices of common construction, sharing electrical characteristics and therefore freely interconnectable.

Two devices of the same family employ the same type of transistor, operate from the same supply, and guarantee the same input and output voltages. Devices within a family may therefore be interconnected without further analysis, whereas connections between families must be verified.

TTL — Transistor–Transistor Logic

Built from bipolar junction transistors and diodes. Runs from a 5 V supply. An input actually draws current, so a driving output has to supply it, and this is what limits how many inputs one output can feed.

Its thresholds sit low in the supply range — guaranteed LOW below 0.8 V, guaranteed HIGH above 2.0 V — which is why an unconnected TTL input tends to float HIGH.

CMOS — Complementary MOS

Built from complementary pairs of MOSFETs, one n-channel and one p-channel. A MOSFET gate is an insulated plate, so a CMOS input draws essentially no steady current and the chip consumes almost no power while it is sitting still.

Its thresholds scale with the supply, around 30% and 70% of VDD, which is why a CMOS part can run from 3 V or 5 V and keep the same proportions.

How the two families behave when connected to one another, and how many loads each can drive, is treated in TTL, CMOS and Interfacing.

Part 3

Packages and pin numbering

Misreading the pin numbering is among the commonest causes of damage to a device on a breadboard.

Dual in-line through-hole package

A DIP has two rows of pins on a 0.1 inch pitch — exactly the spacing of a breadboard, which is why it is the package you build with by hand. One end carries a notch, and sometimes a dot beside pin 1.

Numbering runs counter-clockwise from pin 1, viewed from above. With the notch on the left, pin 1 is the bottom-left pin, the numbers increase along the bottom row to the right, then continue along the top row from right to left. Power and ground land diagonally opposite each other: on a 14-pin logic chip, ground is pin 7 and VCC is pin 14.

Surface-mount parts are smaller and cheaper to assemble by machine, but cannot be inserted into a breadboard. The dual in-line package therefore remains the standard choice for prototyping.
